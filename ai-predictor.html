The Problem
AI displacement is abstract. It shouldn't be.

Most coverage of AI's impact on jobs is alarming but impersonal. Articles talk about percentages of the workforce, entire sectors, broad categories. What they don't tell you is: what about my job specifically? What makes it risky? What can I do about it?

There was no accessible, individual-level tool for this. During my internship at ThinkNeuro, I set out to build one.

What I Built
700+ jobs. Explainable risk scores. Actionable output.

I trained a predictive model on a Kaggle labor market dataset covering 700+ US occupations with automation probability scores. The model classifies each job into Low, Medium, or High risk — but more importantly, it explains why.

The interface was built in Streamlit with Plotly visualizations. Users can search any occupation, see their risk score alongside the factors driving it, compare two careers side-by-side, get skill recommendations to reduce their risk, and explore aggregate trends by job category and employment size.

Technical Architecture
From raw data to deployed app.

The data pipeline uses Pandas for cleaning and feature engineering on the Kaggle dataset. The predictive model outputs both a risk category and a continuous probability score, which feeds the explainability layer. Plotly handles all interactive visualizations. The entire app is deployed on Streamlit Cloud.

The biggest technical challenge wasn't the model — it was making the output interpretable for non-technical users. I rebuilt the UI twice, moving away from raw probability scores toward plain-language explanations and visual risk indicators that anyone could act on.

What I Learned
01
Explainability over accuracy
A model with 90% accuracy that no one understands is less useful than an 80% model that tells users exactly why. I spent more time on the explainability layer than on the model itself.
02
UI is part of the system
Clean results in a Jupyter notebook mean nothing when deployed. I rebuilt the interface twice to make it truly usable for someone without a data science background.
03
The gap between theory and users
This project reinforced a pattern I keep seeing: the hardest part isn't building the system — it's making it fit the people who will actually use it.
