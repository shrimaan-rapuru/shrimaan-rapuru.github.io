The Problem

Small coordination failures create real problems for real people.

Scheduling and communication inefficiencies are common in many volunteer, educational, and organizational environments. Coordinating shifts, tracking availability, and sending reminders often rely on fragmented spreadsheets, group messages, or manual communication, which can create confusion and wasted time.

I became interested in how small operational problems can significantly affect organization and reliability within teams, especially in environments where people depend on timely coordination and communication.

What I Built

A centralized coordination system that reduces manual overhead.

I developed the ODM Scheduling Platform, a centralized scheduling and coordination system designed to streamline volunteer and organizational management. The platform allows users to manage schedules, track participation, organize shift assignments, and improve communication through a more structured digital workflow.

📊 Shift Dashboard
Real-time view of today's appointments, next 7 days, unassigned roles, and scheduling analytics.
📅 Calendar Booking
Month/week/day views with slot availability, appointment creation, urgency levels, and status tracking.
👥 Volunteer Roster
Add and manage volunteers with role assignments, availability settings, and active status.
🔔 Push Notifications
Automated reminders to reduce no-shows and last-minute coordination gaps.

What Happened

I presented it. It wasn't adopted. That was the real lesson.

I presented the platform to ODM's operating director with a full written proposal. The feedback wasn't about the software — it worked. The constraints were organizational: data privacy considerations, staff capacity to onboard a new system, and existing workflows that a new tool would have to integrate with.

"My solution didn't fit a system I hadn't fully understood."

I had arrived with an answer before I fully understood the question. The platform was technically sound, but I had underestimated how much an organization's culture, capacity, and existing processes determine whether a technical solution can actually land.

What I Learned

01
Engineering solves everyday problems
Engineering is often about solving operational problems people encounter every day, even when those problems initially seem small.
02
Functional and intuitive are different
It can be difficult to design systems that are both technically functional and intuitive for users with different needs and backgrounds.
03
Technology should serve people
The project strengthened my interest in designing technology that improves coordination and the overall experience of the people using it.

Future Improvements

  • Database integration — replace session state with a persistent backend so data carries across sessions
  • SMS/email notifications via Twilio or SendGrid for real reminder delivery to volunteers
  • Volunteer self-signup — allow volunteers to claim open shifts from a public-facing view
  • Recurring shift templates for repeating weekly schedules
  • Attendance tracking to build historical reliability data per volunteer
  • Role-based access control with separate admin and volunteer views
