The Problem
Some mornings there were too many volunteers. Other times, not enough.

I joined Open Door Ministries as a volunteer in January 2024, coordinating weekly breakfast service for 75–100 homeless residents. Within weeks, a pattern became obvious: volunteer coordination was chaotic. Some mornings the kitchen was overcrowded; others, understaffed. Food ran short one week and went to waste the next.

There was no system for tracking who was coming, what roles needed filling, or where gaps were forming before they became a problem. As someone with a systems-thinking background, I saw a solvable problem.

What I Built
A complete coordination system — dashboard, calendar, roster, notifications.

I built a four-view web application in Python and Streamlit, designed specifically for ODM's workflow. The dashboard gives a real-time overview of today's shifts, the next 7 days, and any unassigned roles. The calendar view lets staff create appointments with assigned volunteers, urgency levels, and slot types. The volunteer roster manages availability and role assignments. Settings handles users and push notifications.

What Happened
I presented it. It wasn't adopted. That was the real lesson.

I presented the platform to ODM's operating director with a full written proposal. The feedback wasn't about the software — it worked. The constraints were organizational: data privacy considerations, staff capacity to onboard a new system, existing workflows that a new tool would have to integrate with.

"My solution didn't fit a system I hadn't fully understood."

I had arrived with an answer before I fully understood the question. The platform was technically sound, but I had underestimated how much an organization's culture, capacity, and existing processes determine whether a technical solution can actually land.

That experience — building something correct that still didn't fit — shaped how I think about engineering more than any successful implementation would have.

What I Learned
01
Understand before you build
I mapped the technical problem before I fully understood the organizational one. Real solutions require understanding the human system first — its culture, constraints, and capacity — before designing a technical one.
02
Failure is data
The platform not being adopted wasn't a failure of engineering — it was a failure of scoping. That distinction matters. The lesson wasn't "build better software." It was "understand the system you're building for."
03
Technically correct ≠ actually useful
This is the central tension I keep returning to in every project: a solution that works in theory isn't the same as a solution that works for a community. Closing that gap is the work of engineering.
